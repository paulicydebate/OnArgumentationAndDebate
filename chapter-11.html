Objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Explain what cross-examination is for and what it is not for.
  2. Use cross-examination to build credibility, secure links and clarify, in that order of priority.
  3. Build a line of questions using the question ladder.
  4. Answer questions in a way that protects your case without dodging.
  5. Use what you learned in cross-examination in your next speech.

Key terms

Cross-examination, ethos, securing a link, clarification, question ladder, closed question, open question, the fork, pit of doom, reverse pit of doom, concession.

The idea

11.1 What cross-examination is for

Cross-examination (often shortened to "cross-x" or "CX") is the period in a debate when one debater asks questions of the debater who just spoke. In most classroom formats, the questioner is the person who will speak next. It is the only part of a debate where the two sides talk to each other directly instead of to the judge, which makes it the most unpredictable part of the round and, for many students, the most nerve-racking.

The purpose of cross-examination is to look like the debater who best understands the round. That may sound like a strange goal. It means cross-examination is not mainly about getting information, and it is definitely not about winning arguments in the moment. Arguments are won in speeches, where the judge is listening for them and writing them down. Cross-examination sets up those speeches, and it shapes how the judge sees both debaters for the rest of the round.

11.2 Three uses, in order

Cross-examination has three effective uses. They are listed here in order of value, and a strong questioner spends most of the time on the first two.

The first use is ethos, the speaker credibility Chapter 1 introduced. Cross-examination is the only time the judge sees the two debaters engage directly, so it is where credibility shifts fastest. Good questions show that you understand your opponent's case, sometimes better than they do. Questions that reveal your opponent cannot explain their own evidence, or cannot say how their contention connects to the resolution, lower their credibility without a single insult. That last part matters: attacking the person instead of the argument is an ad hominem (Chapter 7), and judges punish it.

The second use is securing a link: asking a question whose answer gives you something to use in your next speech. If you plan to argue that the affirmative's plan costs money, a question that gets them to admit the plan needs new funding has secured your link. Once your opponent has said it, they cannot easily deny it later, and you can quote their answer in your speech.

The third use is clarification: asking about something you genuinely did not understand. Sometimes you have to; you cannot answer an argument you do not understand. But clarification questions work against the main goal, because every one tells the judge you were confused. Keep them short, ask them first, and move on.

At a glance

Use Goal Example question
Ethos Show you understand the round better than your opponent "Can you explain how your second card supports your tag?"
Securing a link Get an answer you can use in your next speech "Your plan needs new federal funding, right?"
Clarification Understand something you missed (use sparingly) "What was your criterion?"

11.3 The question ladder

The biggest mistake new questioners make is asking for the conclusion directly. "Isn't your plan going to cost too much?" invites an easy "No," and now you have helped your opponent deny your argument on the record. A better approach climbs toward the conclusion one small step at a time. Call it the question ladder.

Start at the bottom rung with closed questions, questions with short, factual answers, usually yes or no, that your opponent cannot reasonably deny. "Your plan creates a new federal agency?" "That agency needs staff?" "Staff need salaries?" Each answer is obvious, so your opponent will give it. Each one also commits them to a step in your argument.

Climb one rung at a time, and stop before the top. The last step, "So your plan costs money you haven't accounted for," is the conclusion, and you do not need your opponent to say it. You will say it yourself, in your speech, where the judge is listening for arguments and your opponent cannot interrupt. Asking the final question gives your opponent a chance to deny it or to explain it away. Leaving it unasked lets you draw the conclusion from answers they already gave.

Open questions, questions that invite long answers ("Can you explain your solvency?"), have a place but are risky. They give your opponent time to repeat their case. Use them when you expect the answer to be weak: an open question to a debater who does not understand their own evidence is one of the strongest ethos moves available.

A variation is the fork: a question where either answer helps you. "Does your plan take effect immediately, or does it phase in?" If immediately, the negative argues it is too disruptive; if it phases in, the negative argues the harms continue in the meantime. The fork works only if you have prepared an argument for each answer.

The pit of doom is different. It is a question where one answer clearly gives you access to an argument you are likely to make. Suppose the resolution calls for increasing subsidies for green power, and you have a strong disadvantage about the harms of solar panels, but the affirmative's plan text does not say whether solar is included. Ask: "Do you defend increasing subsidies for solar panels?" A yes hands you the link to your disadvantage. A no tells you something too: that disadvantage will not link, and you can spend your preparation on something else. Unlike the fork, the pit of doom does not need both answers to help you. It needs one answer that does, asked before your opponent works out what is at stake.

Experienced debaters also use the reverse pit of doom. Here the questioner makes the opponent think that answering yes is the link, when the answer that actually gives the link is no. Imagine the negative's real argument is that subsidizing other green power while leaving solar out pulls investment away from solar companies. If the negative asks the solar question in a way that sounds like it is setting up a "solar is harmful" argument, the affirmative may try to dodge by answering no, and that no is exactly the link the negative wanted.

This can go on indefinitely. Debaters who know each other, and know each other's teams, learn each other's habits, and each side tries to work out whether a question is a pit of doom or a reverse one. It works much like poker, where a player has to decide whether an opponent is bluffing, or is strong and only pretending to bluff. For a classroom debate, the lesson is simpler: before you answer a question about what your plan includes, think about which answer the questioner actually wants.

11.4 What not to do

Three habits waste cross-examination time and cost credibility. The first, and the most common, is making arguments instead of asking questions: "Don't you think your evidence is outdated and your plan is a bad idea?" is a speech in disguise, and it hurts you in two ways.

It gives your opponent extra speech time. Cross-examination is your time. If you make an argument instead of asking a question, your opponent can simply answer it, and they answer it on your clock instead of spending their own speech time on it. You have used your resource to do their work.

It probably never reaches the flow. Most judges do not flow cross-examination, even though they usually treat the answers given in it as binding. The danger is in your memory. When you stand up for your speech, you remember making the argument, so you may not make it again. But you made it in cross-examination, so it is unlikely to be on the judge's flow, and it is missing from the part of the debate where it would have counted. Ask the questions in cross-examination, and make the argument in your speech.

The second habit is obscuring the point: long, confusing questions make you look less clear, not your opponent. The third is pressing on parts of your opponent's case that you understood perfectly well but that seem weak. If you understood it, save the attack for your speech, where it counts.

Two more rules keep cross-examination civil. Let your opponent answer; if an answer runs long, you can politely move on ("Thank you, that's helpful; next question"). And speak to the judge as much as to your opponent. You are performing for the person with the ballot.

11.5 Answering questions

Being questioned is half of cross-examination, and it has its own skills. The goal is to look confident and knowledgeable while giving away as little as possible.

Answer honestly and briefly. Short answers give the questioner less to work with. Dodging looks worse than a direct answer; a judge who sees you refusing to answer a simple question assumes the honest answer hurts you.

Watch the ladder. When a series of easy questions seems to be climbing somewhere, you can answer the rung while refusing the destination: "Yes, the agency needs staff, but the plan's funding covers that, as our second card explains." That kind of answer gives the questioner the fact without the conclusion.

Do not make a concession, an admission that gives up part of your case, unless you mean to. If you do not know an answer, say what you do know: "Our evidence doesn't give that number; it says the cost is covered by the existing budget." And when a question is genuinely confusing, ask for it to be repeated. That is not weakness; it is precision.

11.6 Using cross-examination in your speech

Cross-examination only matters if you use it. A concession your opponent made in cross-examination and you never mention in a speech does not exist on the flow. In your next speech, bring it back explicitly: "In cross-examination, they admitted their plan needs new funding. That's the link to our first argument." Quoting your opponent's own words is one of the most persuasive moves in debate, because the judge heard them say it.

Flow cross-examination the same way you flow speeches. Write down the important answers, especially concessions, in the margin next to the argument they affect. Then they are there when you need them.

Say it

  • Bottom rung: "Your plan creates a new agency, correct?"
  • Next rung: "And that agency needs staff?"
  • Moving on politely: "Thank you, that's helpful. Next question."
  • Answering a rung, not the destination: "Yes, but as our second card explains..."
  • Using it later: "In cross-examination, they admitted... That means..."

Worked examples

A personal decision

Resolution: Starting at a community college costs less than starting at a four-year university.

The negative questions the affirmative. "Your evidence compares published tuition?" "Yes." "Published tuition is the price before grants?" "Yes." "Students at four-year schools often get grants?" "Some do." The negative stops there. In its speech, it climbs the last rung on its own: the affirmative's evidence measures sticker price, by its own admission, and the resolution is about what students actually pay. The affirmative answered honestly, which was right, but it missed the chance to answer the destination: "Yes, and our evidence shows the gap stays large even after grants." That answer would have taken the rung without giving up the argument.

A national policy question

Resolution: The United States federal government should substantially restrict the development of artificial intelligence.

The affirmative questions the negative, whose disadvantage claims restrictions will push AI development to other countries. The affirmative uses the fork: "Does your evidence say other countries would develop AI faster, or that they'd develop it with fewer safety rules?" Either answer helps. If faster, the affirmative argues its plan includes international cooperation; if fewer safety rules, it argues that is exactly why U.S. leadership on safety matters. The negative, recognizing the fork, answers precisely: "Our evidence says faster, and it says cooperation agreements have failed before." Precise answers are the best defense against a fork.

How a judge weighs it

Most judges do not decide debates on cross-examination alone, and many do not flow it at all. What judges take from it is an impression of which debater understands the round, and that impression colors how they hear every speech afterward. Concessions count only when a debater brings them into a speech. A judge will not do that work for you.

Common mistakes

  • Asking the final question. Climb the ladder, then draw the conclusion in your speech.
  • Making arguments instead of asking questions. Save arguments for speeches, where the judge flows them and your opponent has to answer them on their own time.
  • Spending the period on clarification. Every confused question costs credibility.
  • Dodging as the answerer. A direct, brief answer looks stronger than an evasive one.
  • Forgetting to use the answers. A concession never mentioned in a speech does nothing.

Practice

Drill: build the ladder (15 minutes, pairs). Pick one argument you want to make against your partner's case. Write a ladder of four closed questions that climbs toward it without asking the conclusion. Run it on your partner, then deliver the conclusion in a thirty-second speech that quotes their answers.

Drill: hot seat (15 minutes, small groups). One student presents a one-minute contention. Each group member asks one question. The presenter practices brief, honest answers that take the rung without giving up the destination. The group names the best answer and why.

Writing exercise. Watch or read a cross-examination from a recorded debate, press conference or hearing. In 200 words, identify one question that worked, one that did not, and explain why using the ideas in this chapter.

Check questions

Choose an answer to see feedback. Nothing is recorded.

1. What is the main purpose of cross-examination?

2. Why should you usually avoid asking the final question in a question ladder?

3. "Does your plan take effect immediately, or does it phase in?" where you have an argument ready for either answer. What is this technique?

4. You're being questioned and see a ladder of easy questions heading toward a damaging conclusion. What's the best response?

5. The resolution calls for increasing subsidies for green power. You have a strong disadvantage about solar panels, but the plan text does not say whether it includes solar. Which question is a pit of doom?

6. Why is making an argument during cross-examination a mistake, even when it is a good argument?

7. Your opponent made a key concession in cross-examination, but you never mention it in a speech. What happens?

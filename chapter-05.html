Objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Name the six elements of effective delivery and adjust each one for a debate.
  2. Choose between manuscript, impromptu and extemporaneous delivery.
  3. Give a roadmap and signpost through a speech.
  4. Recognize three thinking patterns behind communication apprehension and use four strategies to manage it.
  5. Keep a speech within its time limit.

Key terms

Delivery, volume, pace, tone, clarity, fluidity, verbal filler, eye contact, manuscript delivery, impromptu delivery, extemporaneous delivery, roadmap, signposting, communication apprehension.

The idea

5.1 Delivery is part of the argument

Chapter 1 described noise: anything that keeps your message from reaching the judge. In a debate, the biggest source of noise you control is your own delivery. An argument mumbled too fast to flow does not exist for the decision. An argument delivered clearly, at a pace the judge can write down, gets weighed.

That is the reason delivery matters in a debate class. You are not being graded on charisma. You are learning to remove the noise between your arguments and the person deciding.

5.2 Six elements of effective delivery

Delivery can feel like one big skill you either have or do not. It is easier to improve once you break it into parts. This section uses six elements. Each one can be practiced on its own, and each one, done badly, adds a specific kind of noise.

Volume is how loud or soft you speak. The test is simple: can the judge at the back of the room hear every word without leaning in? Nervous speakers tend to get quieter as a speech goes on, and quieter still at the moments that matter most, such as the last line of an argument. Do the opposite. Your most important sentences should be your loudest. Volume also signals confidence. A judge hears a debater who speaks up as someone who believes what they are saying.

Pace is how fast or slow you speak. In a debate, the right pace is the fastest pace at which the judge can still flow, meaning write down your arguments as you make them. That pace depends on the judge, which is why the communication model puts the receiver at the center. The best gauge is the judge's pen. If it stops moving while you are still talking, you have lost them, and everything you say until they catch up is lost too. Vary your pace on purpose: slow down for your claims and your weighing, and move faster through material the judge has already heard.

Tone is the variation in energy, emotion and formality in your voice. A debate calls for a tone that is confident and professional, but not flat. A speech delivered in one unchanging tone makes every argument sound equally important, which means none of them stands out. Let your tone rise when you reach the argument you most want the judge to remember, and stay calm and even when you answer an attack, which tells the judge the attack did not worry you.

Clarity is how easy it is to understand each word you say. It mostly comes down to articulation: finishing your words, hitting final consonants, not letting sentences trail off. Clarity matters more the faster you go. A slow speaker can mumble a little and still be understood; a fast speaker who mumbles is just making sound. If you have to choose between speed and clarity, choose clarity every time.

Fluidity is how naturally your speech flows. The biggest threat to fluidity is verbal filler: sounds and words like "um," "like" and "you know" that fill a gap while your brain catches up. A few are normal. A lot of them make you sound unsure and waste time you do not have. The fix is not to talk faster. It is to pause. A one-second silence sounds deliberate and confident to a listener, even when it feels endless to you.

Eye contact is where you focus your attention while you speak. In a debate, look at the judge, not your opponent. Your opponent is not the one deciding, and arguing at them turns the round into a personal exchange the judge is watching from outside. You will be reading from notes, so you will not hold eye contact the whole time. Aim to look up at the judge at the end of each argument, which is also when you can check their pen.

None of these needs to be perfect. A debater with average delivery and clear structure beats a polished speaker with no structure almost every time. But each element you improve removes noise.

5.3 Three delivery styles

  • Manuscript delivery means reading from a prepared text. It is common in formats where debaters read evidence, and it is the right choice for your first constructive speech, when you have written the case in advance. The risk is reading to the page instead of the judge; look up at the end of each argument.
  • Impromptu delivery means speaking with no preparation. You will do this in cross-examination and whenever you answer something unexpected.
  • Extemporaneous delivery means speaking from an outline after limited preparation. Most rebuttal speeches are extemporaneous: you have notes on your flow and a plan, but you are composing sentences as you go.

A useful rule for beginners: manuscript for the first speech, extemporaneous for everything after. As you get comfortable, you will rely less on the page even in the first speech.

5.4 Roadmaps and signposting

Judges flow, and a judge who knows where you are going can write down everything you say. Two habits make that easy.

A roadmap is a one-sentence preview of the order of your speech, given before the timer starts or in the first seconds. "I'll start on their disadvantage, then go back to our case." Many judges expect one, and it costs you nothing.

Signposting means announcing where you are as you move through the speech. "Now on their first contention." "My second answer." "That takes me to the impact." Every time you move to a new argument, say so.

Signposting is the spoken version of the structure you write. In Chapter 14 you will learn to deliver positions in subordinated notation, with numbered claims and lettered support. Saying the numbers and letters out loud is signposting.

5.5 Managing communication apprehension

Communication apprehension is anxiety connected to speaking. Almost everyone has some. It shows up as a racing heart, a shaky voice or a blank mind, and it is a form of psychological noise. The goal is not to eliminate it, which is rarely possible, but to keep it from getting in the way.

Much of it comes from three thinking patterns:

  • All-or-nothing thinking: believing that a speech that falls short of perfect is a failure.
  • Overgeneralization: treating one bad experience as proof that it will always go badly. "I froze once, so I always freeze."
  • Fortune telling: expecting things to go badly no matter how much you practice.

Noticing which pattern you are in is often enough to loosen its grip. Four strategies help:

  1. Preparation: know your content. Most anxiety before a speech is uncertainty about what you will say.
  2. Practice: run the speech out loud, timed, more than once. Rehearsing in your head is not the same.
  3. Mindfulness: notice the anxiety without judging it. "My heart is racing" is information, not a verdict on your ability.
  4. Grounding: bring your attention back to the present moment. Feel your feet on the floor, take a slow breath, and look at one friendly face.

Debate has one advantage over most public speaking: your judge is trained to listen for arguments, not to judge your nerves. A shaky voice delivering a good argument still wins the argument.

5.6 Time

Every speech in a debate has a time limit, and most judges stop listening when it runs out. Running out of time before you reach your best argument is the same as never making it.

In my classes, the three most common reasons students lose points on their debates are timing, refutation and verbal filler. Each has a simple fix:

  • Timing: time your outline out loud, with a recording, at least three times before the debate.
  • Refutation: write answers to the arguments you expect before the debate, using the four-step frame from Chapter 4.
  • Verbal filler: deliver your first speech from a manuscript, so you never search for words.

In rebuttals, budget your time before you stand up. Decide how many seconds each part of the flow gets, and move on when that time is spent, even if you have more to say.

Say it

  • Roadmap: "I'll start on their case and then go to our advantage."
  • Signpost a new argument: "Now on their second contention."
  • Signpost your answers: "Two answers. First, ___. Second, ___."
  • Signpost a transition inside a position: "That takes me to the impact."
  • Close: "For all these reasons, I urge an affirmative ballot."

A rebuttal opening with a roadmap and signposts sounds like this: "Two places today: their disadvantage, then our case. On the disadvantage, three answers. First, there is no link: their evidence is about federal programs, and our plan is run by the state. Second..."

Worked examples

A campus question

Resolution: This college should replace letter grades with pass/no pass in general education courses.

A student gives the affirmative's first speech. She has written it as a manuscript and timed it three times: it runs four minutes and forty seconds against a five-minute limit, leaving room for a slower pace in the room. Before starting she gives a roadmap ("two advantages: student stress and course exploration"), and she signposts each part as she reaches it. She marks two places on her manuscript where she will look up at the judge and slow down: the end of each advantage's impact. When her voice shakes in the first ten seconds, she pauses, takes a breath and continues. The judge flows every argument.

A federal policy

Resolution: The United States federal government should establish a universal basic income.

A negative debater has four minutes for a rebuttal and four things to cover: the affirmative's two advantages, his own disadvantage, and his final weighing. He budgets the time before standing: one minute for each advantage, one minute extending the disadvantage, one minute weighing. He gives a roadmap in one sentence. Halfway through the second advantage, he sees the minute is spent and moves on, saying "I'll group the rest of their answers here: none of them address our link." He reaches the weighing with a minute left, which is where the debate is decided.

How a judge weighs it

Judges do not usually vote on delivery directly, but delivery decides what reaches the judge's flow, and the flow decides the debate. Clear signposting means your arguments are written in the right place. A pace the judge can follow means nothing is lost. Many judges also award speaker points, a separate score for speaking quality, where delivery, organization and professionalism count directly.

Common mistakes

  • Speaking faster to fit more in. If the judge cannot flow it, it does not count. Cut arguments instead.
  • Skipping the roadmap. It takes five seconds and helps the judge with every argument after it.
  • Reading to the page. Look up at the end of each argument.
  • Filling silence with filler. A pause is fine. "Um" is noise.
  • Not timing practice runs. A speech that runs five minutes in your head often runs six out loud.

Practice

Drill: one-minute signposting (10 minutes, pairs). Each partner gives a one-minute speech answering three made-up arguments. Use a roadmap and signpost every answer. The listener flows the speech and reports any argument they could not place. Switch roles and repeat with no filler words; the listener counts any that slip through.

Writing exercise. Take a speech you have written for this class. Mark your roadmap, every signpost, and the places you will look up and slow down. Time it out loud three times and record the times. In 200 words, describe what you changed after each run.

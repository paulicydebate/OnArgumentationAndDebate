Objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Name the eight elements of the communication model and apply each one to a debate.
  2. Explain the three modes of persuasion and give an example of each.
  3. Define debate and distinguish it from a single argument.
  4. Identify the defining parts of a debate: the resolution, the sides, the judge, and constructive and rebuttal speeches.

The idea

1.1 Arguing is something you already do

You have argued your whole life. You talked a teacher into an extension, convinced a friend to see the movie you wanted, or explained to a parent why the car needed new tires. Each time you had something you wanted another person to believe or do, and you gave them reasons.

This book is about doing that on purpose. Argumentation is the practice of giving reasons to convince someone that an idea or action is right or wrong. Debate is a structured contest in which two sides argue over the same question in front of someone who decides between them. The skills overlap almost completely, which is why a debate class is useful far beyond debate: in a job interview, a city council meeting, or a disagreement at home, you are still building reasons, answering objections and deciding what matters most.

Start with what happens whenever one person tries to reach another.

1.2 The communication model

Every debate is an act of communication, so it helps to have a map of how communication works. The communication model breaks any act of communication into eight elements. It was built to describe communication in general, from a phone call to a television ad, but it maps cleanly onto a debate. To see how, follow one speaker through it. Maya, a community college student, has four minutes to convince a judge that her college should give every student a free transit pass. (This example is hypothetical.)

The sender is the person or source trying to communicate. In a debate, the sender is the debater who has the floor: right now, Maya. The model starts with the sender because communication starts with someone who wants something. Maya wants the judge's ballot, and every other element in the model either helps her get it or gets in her way.

Encoding is turning thoughts into a message: deciding what to say and how to say it. Maya knows more about transit than she can fit in four minutes, so encoding is where she chooses. She leads with students who miss class because the bus costs too much. She cuts the history of the transit district. She explains what "fare-free" means instead of assuming the judge knows. Most of what you will learn in this course, from building arguments to choosing which ones to extend, is a lesson in encoding.

It helps to think of encoding as curation. A museum curator has far more artifacts than the gallery floor can hold, so every exhibit is a set of choices: which pieces go on display, which stay in storage, what order visitors meet them in, and what the placards say. Each choice serves a goal, whether that is teaching visitors something or giving them a particular experience. A debater faces the same problem. You will almost always know more than your speech time can hold, so encoding means curating: picking the arguments and evidence that best serve your goal with this judge, arranging them so the judge meets the most important ones first, and leaving the rest in storage. When Maya cuts the history of the transit district, she is not hiding it. She is deciding it does not earn floor space in this speech.

The message is the information being communicated. In a debate, the message is the arguments, not the speech as a whole. That difference matters. A speech can be long and polished and still carry a thin message, and the judge votes on the message.

The channel is the medium the message travels through. A debate's channel is a spoken speech in a timed format, and that channel shapes everything else. How much the judge can go back and check depends on the format. In an extemporaneous format such as parliamentary debate, the judge's only record of the round is their flow, the notes they write while you speak. A sentence they miss is gone, so a spoken argument has to be clearer and more repetitive than a written one. In an evidence-based format such as Lincoln-Douglas or policy debate, the judge may also have a speech document: a file of the formatted, cited evidence the debaters read from. The judge can reread a piece of evidence, but the document holds the evidence, not your explanation of why it matters, so what you say aloud still decides how the judge understands it. The clock means that every sentence Maya spends on one argument is a sentence she cannot spend on another.

The receiver is the audience that takes in the message. In a debate, the receiver is the judge. The opponent and anyone watching also hear the speech, but only the judge decides. Maya is not trying to persuade her opponent, who was assigned the other side and is not going to change their mind. Still, persuading the judge is not her only obligation. Her opponent is also a receiver: they have to understand her arguments to answer them, and the debate is only fair if they can. An ethical speaker makes reasonable accommodations for the other side, such as slowing down when asked, explaining jargon, and sharing evidence when the format calls for it. Winning because your opponent could not follow you is not the same as winning the argument.

Decoding is how the receiver interprets the message. A judge decodes by listening, writing the argument down and deciding what it means. Decoding is where good encoding pays off or falls apart, and it is where communication most often breaks down, because every receiver interprets a message through their own context. Picture a text from your partner or a close friend that says only "K." If things between you are fine, it reads as a quick, efficient yes. If you argued that morning, the same letter can read as "I am furious with you." The message did not change; the context you brought to it did. Judges decode the same way, through what they already know, believe and expect. If Maya says "transit access improves retention" and the judge does not know that retention means students staying enrolled, the judge decodes a weaker argument than the one Maya meant. Encoding and decoding mirror each other: the sender's job is to make the receiver's job easy.

Feedback is the receiver's response, which tells the sender how the message landed. In conversation, feedback is constant: a nod, a puzzled look, a question. A debate gives you three kinds. The judge's reactions during your speech, such as writing, stopping or frowning, arrive immediately. Your opponent's answers in the next speech show what the other side thinks is weakest. The decision and the judge's comments come last. Strong debaters use all three. If the judge stops writing, slow down. If your opponent spends half a speech on one of your arguments, it probably mattered.

Noise is anything that interferes with the message getting through. It deserves a closer look, because most of what goes wrong in a debate is noise, and it comes in three kinds. Physical noise comes from the room: a hallway conversation, a buzzing projector. Psychological noise comes from inside the people communicating: a debater's anxiety, or a judge who is tired or has already made up their mind. Semantic noise happens when the words themselves get in the way, such as jargon the judge does not know or a term the two sides define differently. Maya's "retention" problem was semantic noise. You cannot remove all noise, but you can reduce it, and Chapter 5 shows how.

Each use of the model happens inside a communication situation, a single, bounded event where communication takes place. For our purposes, a communication situation is one debate. That is why the same speech can win one round and lose the next: the judge, the room and the opponent are different each time.

Of the eight elements, the one that matters most in this class is the receiver. You do not win a debate by being right in the abstract. You win by convincing a specific judge. Every choice you make when encoding, from which arguments to read to how fast to talk, should start from the question "what does this judge need to hear?"

1.3 The three modes of persuasion

More than two thousand years ago, Aristotle described three ways a speaker persuades an audience. They are still the most useful starting point.

  • Ethos is an appeal to credibility: the audience believes you because of who you are or who you cite. "As an emergency room nurse, I have seen what this policy does."
  • Pathos is an appeal to emotion: the audience is moved by what the argument makes them feel. "Imagine telling a child there is no room for her at the shelter tonight."
  • Logos is an appeal to reason: the audience is persuaded by evidence and logic. "Three studies across ten years find the same result."

Good arguments usually use all three. In academic debate, logos carries the most weight, because judges are trained to decide on reasons and evidence. Ethos shows up every time you cite a qualified source, and pathos shows up when you make an impact feel real instead of abstract. A statistic about poverty is logos; describing what poverty does to one family is pathos; citing the Census Bureau for the statistic is ethos.

1.4 From an argument to a debate

An argument is one message inside a communication situation. A debate is the communication situation itself. Here is a working definition:

Debate is a regulated and timed discussion of a proposition between two or more matched sides, decided by an audience, in which one side affirms and the other negates.

Each part of that definition names something every debate has:

  • The resolution. The proposition being debated, also called the topic. "This college should replace letter grades with pass/no pass in general education courses."
  • The sides. The affirmative argues that the resolution should be accepted. The negative argues that it should not. Other formats call them government and opposition, or pro and con.
  • The judge. The person or panel who decides who won, and who often enforces the rules, such as speech times.
  • Constructives and rebuttals. Speeches come in two kinds. In constructive speeches, debaters may make new arguments. In rebuttal speeches, they may not, except to answer something new from the speech just before. That rule keeps the debate fair: nobody gets to introduce an argument the other side never has a chance to answer.

Three other features appear in most debates without being part of the definition. A criterion is the standard the judge uses to decide the round; you will learn the criterion for each kind of resolution in Chapter 3. Flowing is the note-taking system debaters and judges use to track every argument; Chapter 4 teaches it. Cross-examination is a period where debaters question each other directly; it gets its own chapter, Chapter 11.

1.5 What it takes to win

The goal of every debate is simple to state: convince the judge to vote for you. Everything else in this book serves that goal.

That has a useful consequence. If you lose, the most productive thing to believe is that you did not convince that judge, not that the judge was wrong. Maybe your best argument was never explained clearly. Maybe you answered the arguments you expected instead of the ones your opponent made. Treating the decision as feedback, the seventh element of the model, tells you what to change next time. Blaming the judge tells you nothing.

Debating in a class with a judge in the room is practice for any situation where someone else makes the decision: a hiring manager, a jury, a voter, a landlord. In each case, the person deciding is the receiver, and your job is to give them what they need to decide in your favor.

Say it

Most speeches open by telling the judge where they are going. Here are frames you can use from your first practice debate:

  • "I am on the affirmative today, and I am arguing that ___."
  • "The resolution asks whether ___. I will give you two reasons to say yes."
  • "My opponent's case rests on one claim: ___. Here is why that claim is wrong."
  • "When you decide this debate, the question to ask is ___."

A short opening that uses all three modes of persuasion might sound like this: "As a student who works thirty hours a week, I know what a single failed exam costs. The resolution asks whether this college should grade general education courses pass/no pass. I will give you two reasons to say yes: it lowers the stress that drives students to drop out, and it lets them take the courses they are curious about instead of the ones that protect their GPA."

Worked examples

A personal decision

Situation: A friend is deciding whether to start at a community college or a university, and you think the community college is the better choice.

Element Applied
Sender and encoding You decide to lead with cost, because you know your friend worries about money, and to keep it short.
Message Two years at a community college cost far less in tuition, and the credits transfer.
Channel A conversation over lunch, not a group chat, so you can see how they react.
Receiver and decoding Your friend hears "cheaper" and worries you mean "worse."
Noise Psychological: their parents expect a university, and that expectation filters everything you say.
Feedback They push back on prestige, which tells you the next argument has to be about outcomes, not cost.

Notice that the most important information came from feedback. Your first message was not wrong; it answered a question your friend was not asking.

A federal policy

Resolution: The United States federal government should establish a universal basic income.

In a classroom debate on this resolution, the affirmative is the sender and the judge is the receiver. The affirmative encodes the case as two advantages, about poverty and about health. The channel is a timed constructive speech. Noise comes from the affirmative's nerves, from a negative who uses a term like "labor force participation" without explaining it, and from a judge who arrived with doubts about government spending. Feedback arrives in the negative's answers and, finally, in the decision. A debater who thinks about every one of those elements before the round has already done half the work.

How a judge weighs it

A judge cannot weigh an argument they never understood. Before any of the weighing you will learn later, your argument has to survive decoding: the judge has to hear it, understand it and write it down. Judges reward debaters who make that easy by announcing structure, defining terms and speaking clearly. They also notice the modes of persuasion. An argument with a credible source and a real-world consequence carries more weight than a bare assertion, even when the logic is the same.

Common mistakes

  • Arguing to the opponent instead of the judge. Your opponent is not going to concede. Every sentence is for the person with the ballot.
  • Treating noise as someone else's problem. Speed, jargon and nerves are all noise you can reduce.
  • Ignoring feedback. A judge who stops writing or looks confused is telling you something. Slow down, explain, or move on.
  • Relying on one mode of persuasion. Pure emotion feels manipulative, and pure statistics can feel abstract. Combine them.
  • New arguments in a rebuttal. Unless you are answering something new from the previous speech, a judge will ignore it.

Practice

Drill: three audiences (15 minutes, groups of three). Each group picks one scenario: convincing someone not to drop out of high school, asking a boss for a raise, or asking a professor for an extension. Write a 30-second appeal. Then rewrite it twice: once for a receiver who trusts you completely, once for a receiver who is skeptical of you. Mark which mode of persuasion each version leans on, and present the three versions to the class.

Writing exercise. Describe a real argument you had in the last month, in 200 to 300 words, using all eight elements of the communication model. Identify the main source of noise and one thing you could have changed in your encoding.

Check questions

Choose an answer to see feedback. Nothing is recorded.

1. In a debate, who is the receiver whose decoding matters most?

2. A judge does not know what "solvency" means, so they miss your best argument. What kind of noise is this?

3. "As a nurse with twenty years in the emergency room, I have seen this happen." Which mode of persuasion is this?

4. In which kind of speech are brand-new arguments not allowed, except to answer something new from the previous speech?

5. Which is not part of the definition of debate?

Move: All four. Builds on: Chapters 1 through 19. Where you use it: after this course, in competitive debate if you choose it, and in work, civic life and your own decisions whether you do or not.

Objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Describe how the classroom debates in this book relate to the major competitive debate formats.
  2. Identify which competitive format fits your interests.
  3. Apply the four moves to arguments at work.
  4. Apply them to civic decisions and public arguments.
  5. Apply them to your own decisions.

Key terms

Competitive debate, policy debate, Lincoln-Douglas debate, parliamentary debate, public debate, forensics, the four moves.

The idea

20.1 What you have learned

This book has been organized around four moves. To build is to construct an argument with a claim, a warrant and an implication, whether as a contention, an advantage or a disadvantage. To prove is to support each part with research and evidence that would survive your opponent reading the whole source. To answer is to respond to one part of an opponent's argument at a time, with four-step refutation, B.U.T.T.O.N. or the algebra. And to weigh is to tell a judge why what you won matters more than what you lost.

You have practiced the four moves in three kinds of debate on one subject: a fact debate about what is true, a value debate about what matters, and a policy debate about what to do. Those three questions, in that order, sit underneath almost every serious decision people make. That is why the moves outlast the course.

20.2 Competitive debate

Competitive debate is debate as an activity, with tournaments, teams and coaches. Many colleges, including many community colleges, have debate teams, usually as part of a forensics program that also includes individual speaking events. Debate formats differ in how many people are on a side, how much research is done in advance, and who judges, but every one of them uses the four moves.

Policy debate is the format closest to Part V of this book. Teams of two debate one policy resolution for an entire season, which allows deep research and large evidence files. Judges are usually experienced, and the debates are often fast and highly technical. If you enjoyed researching and building advantages and disadvantages, this is the format that rewards it most.

Lincoln-Douglas debate pits one debater against another. In high school, it is usually a value debate, close to Chapter 11. At the college level, the version run by the National Forensic Association is a one-person policy format with evidence, combining the research of Part V with a faster pace for a single debater.

Parliamentary debate gives debaters a new resolution at each round and a short preparation time, usually without prepared evidence. It rewards broad knowledge, quick thinking and the ability to make and explain arguments without cards, which is why Chapter 9 stressed explaining the argument a card would contain. Parliamentary resolutions can be fact, value or policy, so all three parts of this book apply.

Public debate formats are built around persuading a general audience. Rounds are often judged by people without debate experience, so the skills of Chapter 10 (audience analysis, clear explanation and plain language) matter most. If you liked debating for lay judges, look for these formats.

Whichever format interests you, the path in is the same: ask your instructor whether your college has a team. Most teams welcome beginners, and the skills from this course are a strong foundation.

20.3 Arguing at work

Most workplace arguments are policy debates in disguise. A proposal to change a schedule, buy new software or hire another person is an affirmative case: there is a harm, a barrier, a plan and an advantage. A manager weighing the proposal is a judge, usually a lay one. The habits from this course transfer directly. Lead with the claim. Support it with evidence your audience trusts. Anticipate the objections and pre-empt the strongest ones (Chapter 16). Compare costs and benefits explicitly, using the weighing terms of Chapter 10. And when someone pushes back, answer the warrant of their objection rather than repeating yourself.

The same skills help when you are the one deciding. Asking "what is the internal link here?" or "is this already happening without the change?" are two of the most useful questions in any meeting.

20.4 Arguing in civic life

Every ballot measure, school board decision and city budget is a policy resolution, and every campaign is an argument aimed at lay judges: you. The skills that make you a better debater make you harder to fool. Trace a viral claim back to its source (Chapter 8). Ask whether an alarming statistic is unique to the policy being debated or would happen anyway (Chapter 14). Notice a false dilemma or a slippery slope in a political ad (Chapter 7). Ask what the strongest argument on the other side is, and whether the people you agree with have answered it.

Debate also teaches something harder: arguing for a position you do not hold, and seeing why reasonable people hold it. That does not mean every position is equally good. It means you understand the disagreement well enough to judge it, which is what civic life asks of everyone.

20.5 Arguing with yourself

The individual-level examples in this book, such as starting at a community college or choosing a major, were chosen because the same moves work on personal decisions. A big decision has a fact question (what is actually true about my options?), a value question (what matters most to me?) and a policy question (what should I do?). Answering them in that order, with evidence, and with the strongest case for the option you are leaning against, is about as good a decision method as there is.

This book began with the idea that argument is how people decide things together. It ends with the same idea, applied to the hardest audience you will ever face: yourself.

Say it

  • At work: "The problem is... it hasn't been fixed because... here's what I propose... and here's why it's worth the cost."
  • In a meeting: "Is that already happening without the change?"
  • About a claim online: "Where does that number come from?"
  • About a decision: "What's the strongest case for the option I'm leaning against?"

Worked examples

A personal decision

A student finishing this course is deciding whether to join the college debate team. Fact: the team practices twice a week and travels to several tournaments a year. Value: the student cares most about building skills for a career in law. Policy: join the team for one season. The student weighs the time cost against the benefit, uses the even-if from Chapter 13 ("even if it takes time from work, the skills matter more for my goal"), and asks the coach about the commitment before deciding. The four moves did not make the decision. They made it a clear one.

A civic question

A city proposes a sales tax to fund road repairs. A resident applies the book. Harms: are the roads actually in poor condition, according to a source with no stake in the vote? Inherency: why hasn't the existing budget fixed them? Solvency: does the measure guarantee the money goes to roads? Disadvantages: who pays the tax, and does it fall hardest on people with less? Weighing: which matters more in this city, safer roads or the cost to the residents least able to pay? The resident still has to decide. But now they know what they are deciding.

How a judge weighs it

Outside the classroom, the judges are managers, voters, juries, committees and you. They rarely announce their criteria, and they rarely write ballots. But they decide the way the judges in this book did: by asking who made the clearest case, who answered the other side, and why what they won matters more. Everything you have practiced is preparation for them.

Common mistakes

  • Treating debate as only a classroom skill. The four moves work everywhere arguments are made.
  • Debating a lay audience like a technical one. Most real-world judges are lay judges. Adapt.
  • Only seeking out arguments you already agree with. The strongest case against your view is the most useful one to know.
  • Waiting to be asked. If competitive debate interests you, ask about your college's team.

Practice

Drill: everyday resolution (15 minutes, small groups). Each student brings one decision from work, school or community life. The group turns it into a fact, a value and a policy resolution, then names the strongest argument on each side of the policy resolution.

Writing exercise. Choose a current ballot measure, local decision or workplace proposal. In 300 words, analyze it using the stock issues from Chapter 15 and the weighing terms from Chapter 10, and end with the strongest argument on the side you do not support.

Check questions

Choose an answer to see feedback. Nothing is recorded.

1. Which competitive format gives debaters a new resolution each round and usually no prepared evidence?

2. A coworker proposes buying new software. In debate terms, what is the proposal?

3. A viral post claims a policy will cause a huge harm. Which question from this book helps most?

4. In what order should a big personal decision be approached, according to this book?

5. Which habit from debate makes you hardest to fool in civic life?

Move: Build. Builds on: Chapters 3 and 14. Where you use it: writing and defending an affirmative case in any policy debate, and answering one as the negative.

Objectives

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  1. Name the six stock issues of policy debate and explain what each requires.
  2. Write a clear plan text that answers "who should do what?"
  3. Distinguish structural, attitudinal and existential inherency, and explain why only two of them are worth arguing.
  4. Use the fact and value debates to supply the harms and the impact weighing for a policy case.
  5. Build and answer a topicality argument in four parts.
  6. Explain topicality as a division of ground, and tell proven abuse from potential abuse.
  7. Choose topicality standards that win, including the difference between how predictable an interpretation is and how predictable it makes the debate.
  8. Answer topicality completely, and decide when the negative should go for it.

Key terms

The idea

15.1 The big question

Every policy resolution asks one question: who should do what? "The United States federal government should substantially restrict the development of artificial intelligence" names an actor (the federal government), a direction (restrict) and a subject (AI development). The affirmative's job is to answer the question with a specific proposal and prove that the world is better with it than without it.

Chapter 6 introduced stock issues as the minimum elements an affirmative needs to win. Policy debate has six. Debaters often remember them as SHIPSAT: Significant Harms, Inherency, Plan, Solvency, Advantages and Topicality. An affirmative that fails any one of them has not proved the resolution should be adopted.

15.2 Harms and inherency

Significant harms are the problems that exist in the status quo because the plan has not been adopted. They are the reason anyone should care about the resolution. Harms must be significant, meaning large enough to justify changing policy; a minor inconvenience does not justify a new federal law. In the Uniqueness Model from Chapter 14, the harms live in the uniqueness of each advantage: the description of what is going wrong right now.

Inherency is the barrier that keeps the status quo from solving the harms on its own. It answers the question every judge silently asks: if this is such a good idea, why hasn't it happened already? There are three kinds.

Structural inherency is a law, rule or institution that blocks the solution or causes the harm. "Current federal law includes no licensing requirement for training the largest AI systems" is structural inherency: the gap is built into the system.

Attitudinal inherency is a belief or attitude among decision-makers that prevents the solution: "Congress has been unwilling to regulate AI because members fear slowing American innovation." Attitudes can change, so attitudinal inherency is weaker than structural inherency, but it is legitimate when you have evidence that the attitude exists and is the reason for inaction.

Existential inherency argues that because the harm exists, the status quo must be unable to solve it. That is circular: it uses the harm as proof of the barrier. It is the weakest kind of inherency and not one you should rely on. If you cannot name the structure or attitude blocking a solution, you probably have not researched the topic enough yet.

15.3 Plan and solvency

The plan is the specific action the affirmative proposes. It is written as a plan text, one or two sentences that state exactly who does what. A good plan text names the actor from the resolution, describes the action precisely enough to debate, and stays within the resolution. For the AI resolution: "The United States federal government should require a federal license before any company trains an artificial intelligence model above a set computing threshold." (This plan is an example for this book, not a description of current law.)

Plan texts are short on purpose. They do not explain why the plan is good; the rest of the case does that. They say what the affirmative will defend, so the negative knows what it is arguing against. Vague plans make debates worse, because the affirmative can shift what it means in every speech. A clear plan text is a commitment.

Solvency is the argument that the plan actually fixes the harms. It is the link and internal link of each advantage from Chapter 14: the plan does this, and that changes things in this way. Solvency needs evidence. A judge who believes the harms are real and the plan is legal still will not vote for a plan that would not work. Strong solvency evidence is specific to the plan's mechanism: evidence that licensing systems have reduced risk in other industries is better than evidence that "regulation helps."

Solvency does not have to be total. A plan that solves most of a harm can still be worth adopting, as long as what it solves outweighs any disadvantages.

15.4 Advantages

An advantage is an independent reason the world of the plan is better than the status quo. Each advantage is a full Uniqueness Model: uniqueness (the harm now), link (the plan), internal link (how the plan changes things) and impact (why it matters to people). "Independent" means each advantage can win the debate on its own. A case with two advantages makes the negative answer both; if the negative beats one, the other can still carry the round. Chapter 16 covers how to write advantages.

15.5 What the fact and value debates supply

This book's sequence puts the policy debate last for a reason. By the time students debate whether the federal government should restrict AI development, they have already debated whether AI will cause more job creation than displacement, and whether AI does more harm than good. Those debates hand the policy debate two things.

The fact debate supplies harms. The evidence about job displacement, privacy or safety risks that students tested in the fact debate becomes the uniqueness of an affirmative advantage: this is what is happening now. Students already know which of that evidence held up under attack and which did not.

The value debate supplies weighing. The arguments about which impacts matter most (poverty against innovation, irreversible harm against temporary disruption) become the impact calculus of the policy debate. An affirmative that won the argument that irreversible harms come first can use it to weigh its advantages against the negative's disadvantages.

That is why the sequence matters even outside this book's examples. Every policy decision rests on factual claims about what is happening and value claims about what matters. The policy debate is where they meet.

At a glance

Stock issue What it requires AI example
Significant harms A real, sizable problem in the status quo Rapidly growing AI systems with no safety review
Inherency A barrier keeping the status quo from solving it No federal licensing law exists (structural)
Plan A specific action: who does what Federal license required above a computing threshold
Solvency Evidence the plan fixes the harm Licensing has reduced risk in other high-risk industries
Advantages Independent reasons the plan makes the world better Fewer catastrophic AI failures; protection for workers
Topicality The plan falls within the resolution Licensing is a substantial restriction on development

15.6 Topicality

Topicality is the requirement that the plan fall within the resolution. It is the affirmative's burden, but it usually only becomes an issue when the negative argues the plan is not topical. A plan to restrict AI development is topical on a resolution about restricting AI development; a plan to fund AI research is not, because it does the opposite. Most topicality debates are closer than that, and they are decided by a four-part argument the negative presents and the affirmative answers.

The interpretation is the negative's definition of a word or phrase in the resolution, usually from a source. "'Restrict' means to place a legal limit on an activity." The best interpretations come from sources that define the term in exactly the context of the resolution. A definition that explicitly excludes what the affirmative does is stronger than a general dictionary entry, and a definition from the field (a legal source for a legal term) is stronger than one from a general source.

The violation explains how the affirmative's plan fails to meet the interpretation. "The plan requires a license but sets no limit; companies that apply will receive one, so nothing is restricted." The violation should be short and specific.

The standards are the reasons the negative's interpretation is better than the affirmative's. Common standards include predictability (could the negative have expected this plan from the resolution's wording?), limits (does the interpretation keep the number of possible plans manageable?) and field context (does the definition come from experts in the topic?).

The voters are the reasons topicality should decide the debate. Topicality is usually argued to come first, because a judge cannot decide whether a plan is a good idea under the resolution until they decide whether it is under the resolution at all. The common voters are fairness, because the negative could not prepare for plans outside the resolution, and education, because debates focused on the resolution teach more about its subject.

The affirmative answers topicality in the same structure: it argues that it meets the negative's interpretation anyway (often the shortest and best answer), offers its own interpretation that it does meet, and explains why its interpretation is better, or at least reasonable. Many judges give the affirmative the benefit of the doubt if its interpretation is reasonable, even if the negative's is slightly better.

Two special cases have their own names. A plan is extra-topical when it does what the resolution asks and then adds something the resolution does not include. On the AI resolution, a plan that licenses AI training and also pays for worker retraining is extra-topical: retraining is not a restriction on development. The problem is that the affirmative chose the extra piece, so it will always be one that helps the affirmative, and the negative could not have prepared for it. A plan is effects topical when its action is not what the resolution describes, but its results might be. A plan to raise electricity prices for data centers does not restrict AI development; it only makes development more expensive and hopes a restriction follows. The more steps there are between the plan and the resolution, the fewer of the negative's prepared links apply.

15.7 What topicality is really about

Topicality is usually described as a limit on the affirmative, but it is better understood as a division of ground: the arguments each side is entitled to make. The affirmative gets its plan, and the negative gets everything else. There is no negative resolution, so the negative can answer with disadvantages, counterplans or any other argument that clashes. The resolution is what tells the negative which plans to prepare for. When the affirmative steps outside it, the affirmative either takes ground that belonged to the negative or shrinks the negative's ground by making its prepared arguments irrelevant.

Negatives call this abuse, and there are two kinds. Proven abuse is ground the negative actually lost in this debate: a disadvantage it read no longer links, or a counterplan it prepared no longer makes sense. Potential abuse is ground the affirmative's interpretation would let affirmatives take, even if it did not happen here. Most judges want proven abuse, and some will not vote on topicality without it. The best case for potential abuse is about preparation: even if no argument failed to link, not knowing what the affirmative might defend limited which arguments the negative could prepare and choose to read. Expect to lose that argument more often than you win it. The usual reply is that voting on potential abuse is like voting on a disadvantage nobody read: the affirmative could have done something unfair, but it did not.

Not everyone accepts that topicality is about ground. Todd Graham and Brandon Merrell, writing in the journal Parliamentary Debate in 2016, argued that the test of topicality should be accuracy: whether the plan fits what the resolution's words actually mean. They point out a problem with the ground view. If topicality is only about protecting the negative's ground, the best interpretation is always the narrowest one, ideally a resolution with exactly one topical plan, even if every expert in the field defines the term differently. On the accuracy view, neither team wrote the resolution, and an affirmative that defends the most accurate meaning of its words has done its job even if that meaning leaves the negative little ground.

Most judges still treat topicality as a question of ground, so argue it that way by default. An affirmative that is losing the ground debate, though, can shift to accuracy. The only way to win accuracy is with field context: evidence that experts in the subject define the term the way the affirmative does, and an explanation of why that source is the best authority on the term.

15.8 Standards that win

Most topicality debates are decided at the level of standards, so choose ones you can defend. Think of each standard as an internal link: it explains how an interpretation leads to the voters, fairness and education.

A standard has to pass one test before it counts: the benefit must come from your interpretation and not from the other side's. This is the same idea as uniqueness in Chapter 14. Suppose an affirmative says its interpretation is better because it lets students learn about AI safety. If plans about AI safety are also topical under the negative's interpretation, both interpretations produce that benefit, and it cancels. Before you answer a standard, ask whether your opponent's interpretation is really the only one that provides it. Before you read one, write your interpretation so that it is.
For the negative, two standards do most of the work. Predictable limits is the argument that the negative can prepare only if the set of topical plans is both manageable and knowable before the debate. "Limits" alone is not enough, because an affirmative can always answer that its own interpretation is even more limiting: it allows only the affirmative's plan. What matters is whether the negative could have known the limit in advance. Field context is the argument that the definition comes from experts in the resolution's subject. It is usually the easiest standard to win, because it reaches both voters: expert definitions teach the subject, and they are the ones a prepared debater would expect.

That leads to the distinction topicality debates most often miss. The predictability of an interpretation is different from the predictability an interpretation produces. Suppose a criminal justice resolution has two interpretations of one of its terms. One comes from the Supreme Court and allows fifteen kinds of plans. The other comes from a stranger's blog and allows two. Once you know the blog's definition, the debate is very predictable: only two plans, and you know the disadvantages to both. But no one preparing for the topic would have found that blog. Predictability that exists only after the debate starts does not help the negative prepare, so the interpretation the negative could have predicted, the Supreme Court's, is better. The same answer beats a common affirmative trick, the counter-interpretation that is "your interpretation plus our plan." Adding one plan sounds harmless, but the negative could not have known which plan would be added.

For the affirmative, the strongest standards are affirmative flexibility and field context. Affirmative flexibility argues that the affirmative needs room to be creative, because the negative is almost never constrained: it can read disadvantages, counterplans and other arguments against any plan, while the affirmative is bound by the resolution. As long as a plan stays within the resolution, unexpected plans are good for debate. Flexibility is a better version of a "creativity" standard. Creativity claims only education; flexibility also claims fairness, because it balances the negative's freedom.

The negative's best answer to flexibility is that limits produce creativity. A designer told to build "anything" has less to work with than one told to build a phone that fits in a pocket and costs under a set price. An affirmative that has to find its best plan inside the resolution is doing harder and more creative work than one that leaves it.
At one national college tournament, the resolution called for increasing the U.S. naval presence in the Persian Gulf. Most teams expected plans that sent warships, and prepared disadvantages about provoking Iran. One team's plan sent the Navy's two hospital ships, the USNS Comfort and USNS Mercy, which each hold about 1,000 hospital beds, carry no weapons and are painted with large red crosses. The plan plainly increased naval presence in the Gulf, so it was topical, and it made the expected disadvantages very hard to win. That is affirmative flexibility at work: unexpected, but inside the resolution.

15.9 Answering topicality and going for it

Topicality is easiest to debate if you treat it as a disadvantage to the affirmative's interpretation, built from the same parts as the Uniqueness Model from Chapter 14:

Topicality Works like What it does
Interpretation Uniqueness Describes debate as it should be under the resolution
Violation Link Shows the plan breaks the interpretation
Standards Internal link Explain how the violation hurts debate
Voters Impact Fairness and education
We meet No-link The plan meets the interpretation, so the violation disappears
Counter-interpretation Counterplan An alternative definition that claims standards for itself

The comparison tells each side what to do. A we meet is a no-link: if the plan meets the negative's interpretation, the topicality argument is over, which is why it comes first and is often the best answer. A counter-interpretation is a counterplan: it offers another way to define the term, and like a counterplan it is worth reading only if it wins standards the negative cannot reach or takes the negative's standards for itself. A counter-interpretation that is not more predictable, better limited or better grounded in the field gives the judge no reason to prefer it.

A complete affirmative answer usually has six parts: we meet; a counter-interpretation; reasons the counter-interpretation is good; reasons the negative's interpretation is bad; no voting on potential abuse; and reasonability. Reasonability asks the judge to vote affirmative as long as the plan is a reasonable reading of the resolution, even if the negative's interpretation is slightly better. Its opposite, competing interpretations, asks the judge to adopt whichever interpretation wins on the flow, however small the margin. Explain what "reasonable" means by giving the judge a test, for example: "If the plan sounded like an example of the resolution when you first heard it, the negative could have expected it too." Otherwise the negative will argue that reasonability is arbitrary and invites the judge to substitute their own opinion. The best way to win reasonability, though, is to be plainly topical. Teams that write plans squarely inside the resolution rarely lose on topicality.

For the negative, whether to go for topicality depends on those six answers. If the affirmative misses three or more of them, topicality is usually the strongest argument in the debate, and the negative can decide as soon as the affirmative finishes answering it. If the affirmative answers all of them well, topicality is an uphill fight that makes sense only when the negative is behind everywhere else. To go for it, extend it like a disadvantage: the interpretation, one clear violation with reasons each we meet fails, one or two standards, and the voters, and win competing interpretations. Because the negative will almost always go for predictable limits or field context, those explanations can be written and practiced before the tournament, with a blank for the topic-specific details.

Topicality is the most common example of a theory argument: an argument about the rules of the debate instead of the merits of the plan. Any theory argument uses the same four parts. An interpretation states the rule, a violation shows the other side broke it, standards explain why the rule makes debates better, and voters explain why breaking it should decide the round. A negative that thinks a plan text is too vague to debate (section 15.3) would argue it in exactly this structure.

Say it

  • Plan text: "The United States federal government should..."
  • Inherency: "The status quo hasn't solved this because..."
  • Solvency: "The plan fixes this because..."
  • Topicality, negative: "Interpretation... Violation... Standards... Voters..."
  • Topicality, affirmative: "We meet their interpretation, because..."
  • Predictability: "The question isn't whether their interpretation makes the debate predictable once we know it. It's whether we could have predicted their interpretation before the round."
  • Reasonability: "If our plan sounded like the resolution when you first heard it, we're reasonably topical. Don't vote on a definition that is better by a hair."
  • Proven abuse: "They can't name an argument they lost. Voting on potential abuse is like voting on a disadvantage nobody read."

Worked examples

A personal decision

Resolution: Students who are undecided about a major should start at a community college.

The affirmative's harms come straight from the earlier fact debate: tuition at four-year universities is far higher, and undecided students often change majors, paying for courses that do not count toward their eventual degree. Its inherency is attitudinal: many students and families believe a four-year start is the only respectable path. Its plan: an undecided student enrolls at a community college for two years with a guaranteed transfer agreement. Its solvency: transfer agreements protect credits, and lower tuition limits the cost of exploring. The negative runs topicality: "undecided" means a student with no intended major, while the affirmative's evidence covers students with a tentative major. The affirmative answers that it meets the interpretation, since its plan applies to students with no declared major, and the debate moves on to the advantages.

A national policy question

Resolution: The United States federal government should substantially restrict the development of artificial intelligence.

The affirmative presents the licensing plan above. Its first advantage uses safety harms the class researched during the value debate; its second uses the job-displacement evidence from the fact debate, arguing that licensing slows deployment enough for retraining to keep up. Inherency is structural: no federal licensing law exists. The negative argues topicality: a license that any company can obtain is not a "substantial" restriction. The affirmative answers that its plan includes safety requirements that some companies will not meet, so it does limit development, and that the negative's interpretation of "substantial" would allow almost no plans at all. The judge decides whether that interpretation is reasonable before turning to the advantages.

How a judge weighs it

A judge checks the stock issues as a series of gates. Is there a significant harm? Is there a barrier in the status quo? Does the plan solve? Is it topical? An affirmative that clearly loses one of these, especially solvency or topicality, usually loses the debate regardless of how large its impacts are. Past the gates, the judge weighs the advantages against whatever the negative has won, using the impact calculus from Chapter 10. That comparison is the net benefits criterion from Chapter 3: the judge votes for whichever world, with the plan or without it, is better.

Common mistakes

  • Relying on existential inherency. Name the law, structure or attitude that blocks a solution.
  • Vague plan texts. If the plan does not say who does what, the debate has no fixed target.
  • Solvency evidence that is too general. Evidence should be about the plan's mechanism, not regulation in general.
  • Ignoring topicality until the last speech. Answer it the first time it is read.
  • Arguing limits without predictability. Any affirmative can claim its own interpretation is the most limiting.
  • A counter-interpretation that wins nothing. It should claim standards the negative cannot reach, or take the negative's.
  • Going for topicality against complete answers. If the affirmative answered every part well, look for a better argument.
  • Calling a voter a standard. Fairness and education are the voters. A standard explains how your interpretation produces them.
  • Forgetting the earlier debates. The fact and value debates already supplied your harms and your weighing. Use them.

Practice

Drill: plan text clinic (15 minutes, small groups). Each student writes a plan text for the AI policy resolution. The group tests each one: does it name the actor, state the action precisely, and fall within the resolution? Rewrite until all three are true.

Drill: topicality in four (15 minutes, pairs). One partner writes a four-part topicality argument against the other's plan text. The other answers with "we meet" if possible, then a counter-interpretation. Switch.

Drill: predictable or not (10 minutes, small groups). The instructor gives each group a term from a resolution and two definitions: one from an expert source, and one from an obscure source that allows fewer plans. The group prepares a one-minute negative argument for the expert definition, using the difference between how predictable a definition is and how predictable it makes the debate. The obscure definition's group answers with limits.

Writing exercise: your topicality blocks. Write a one-minute script that extends predictable limits to fairness and education, with a bracketed blank for the topic-specific details. Practice delivering it five times, filling the blank with a different resolution each time.

Writing exercise. Write a complete affirmative outline for the AI policy resolution: harms (from the fact debate), inherency, plan text, solvency, and one advantage in the Uniqueness Model, with a sentence on how the value debate's weighing applies.
